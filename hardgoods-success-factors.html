What Makes a Supplier Convert & Stick

Success factors from our onboarded financing clients — the patterns in the data and the logic behind them.
Basis: 139 onboarded hardgoods suppliers · 14,048 financing transactions · Jan 2024 – Jun 2026. Stickiness measured on the 97 suppliers with at least 12 months of track record.

Bottom line

  • How a supplier trades predicts success far better than how big they are. The strongest clients ship many small invoices frequently; the weakest ship a few large ones.
  • Volume is a trap. Our worst-performing group actually had higher monthly volume than a mid group — but churned within ~6 weeks. We should not chase leads on headline size.
  • Seven factors separate winners from churners: shipment frequency, invoice structure, product category, growth momentum, product breadth, buyer quality (selling to major US retailers), and country / export orientation. These are all visible before we finance — in customs / 1688 data.
  • Several factors behave differently by category — product breadth is decisive for textiles and tableware but not decor; a US-retailer buyer matters most for tableware and decor. The read must be category-aware, not one-size-fits-all.
  • One high-value persona — frequent small-ticket exporters — is only 11% of our client count but 64% of financed value. Finding more of them is the prize.

1Shipment frequency — the single strongest signal

How often a supplier ships beats every other metric at predicting a lasting relationship.

We grouped clients by how many financing transactions they did in their first 90 days. The pattern is steep and consistent: the more frequently a supplier transacts early, the more likely they are still active a year or more later.

16+ shipments / 90d88%
6–15 shipments / 90d35%
3–5 shipments / 90d19%
1–2 shipments / 90d11%
Bars = share of suppliers still active / long-tenured (our "stickiness" measure).
Why it works: a supplier who ships often has a continuous working-capital need — that is exactly the cash-flow gap our financing fills. They embed the facility into their operating rhythm. An occasional shipper takes financing once and drifts away.

2Small, frequent invoices > large, occasional ones

The same monthly volume can be a great client or a terrible one — depending on invoice structure.

Sticky clients have a median invoice around $2.6K; churned clients sit near $18K. Suppliers whose typical invoice exceeds $50K have only 8% stickiness (92% churn). Critically, total monthly flow was almost identical between sticky and churned groups (~$58–66K/month) — so size tells us nothing on its own.

The volume trap: Two suppliers each move $60K/month. Supplier X does 20 invoices of $3K → sticks. Supplier Y does 2 invoices of $30K → gone in weeks. If BD ranks leads by headline volume, it will systematically pick the wrong ones.
Why it works: frequent small invoices mean a diversified, recurring, operational financing relationship. A couple of large invoices is a one-off event — high concentration, no rhythm, no stickiness.

Home textiles, decor and tableware — small-ticket, high-frequency home goods — are our strongest verticals. Furniture is weak (big-ticket, infrequent). A large cluster of Chinese component / electronics manufacturers (~38 accounts) had zero sticky clients — they are a different business entirely and should not be treated as target leads.

Composition note: of the 139 accounts, only ~59 are true home-goods suppliers. 38 are component/electronics manufacturers and 21 are apparel — different verticals with different economics. The success pattern below is drawn from the home-goods core.

Why it works: growth is also timing. A supplier scaling up faces a widening gap between production outlay and buyer payment — the moment financing becomes most attractive. Momentum signals both quality and readiness to buy.

India and Hong Kong suppliers are our strongest by country; the current China cohort is weak, largely reflecting its category mix (many were component makers, not home goods). Suppliers with no export flow have never been sticky — there are no cross-border receivables to finance — so an export orientation is a baseline requirement, not a plus.

6Product breadth — a real relationship ships many products

How many distinct products a supplier ships separates a genuine manufacturing partner from a one-off deal.

Suppliers shipping a wide range of products are far more sticky than those shipping just a few — and this holds even when we compare suppliers who ship equally often, so it is a signal in its own right, not just a by-product of frequency.

50+ distinct products89%
11–50 products73%
4–10 products27%
1–3 products32%
Stickiness by number of distinct products shipped.

This differs by category — sticky vs churned suppliers, median distinct products: Home Textile 26 vs 5, Kitchen & Dining 14 vs 5, Furniture 12 vs 2. In Home Decor both groups ship broad ranges, so breadth matters less there — decor is product-heavy by nature.

Why it works: a broad, evolving product line signals an established factory with ongoing buyer programs — repeat, renewing demand. A one- or two-product supplier is often fulfilling a single order, with no continuing financing need once it ships.

Suppliers whose buyers are large US retailers (Walmart, Target, TJX, Ross, Gap, Lowe's and similar) are dramatically more sticky. The effect is strongest in Kitchen & Dining (0.75 vs 0.33) and Home Decor (0.88 vs 0.50); Home Textile suppliers tend to be sticky regardless of destination.

Why it works: a blue-chip retail buyer means stable, recurring purchase orders and a creditworthy receivable — exactly the anchor that keeps a financing relationship alive and safe to underwrite.

Product breadth (5) is decisive for textile / tableware / furniture but less so for decor; buyer quality (6) matters most for tableware & decor, while textile suppliers stay sticky regardless of buyer. Read each lead against its own category, not a single global bar.

When the seven factors combine, clients fall into four recognizable profiles. Stickiness and value concentrate sharply in the top one.

The top profile is only 11% of accounts but 64% of financed value, and by far the stickiest. The bottom profile looks sizeable by monthly volume yet churns at 96% — essentially a one-off transaction that leaves within weeks. Our BD priority should be finding more of the top profile — and not being seduced by the bottom one.

9The logic — how we'd turn this into lead prioritization

These same factors are observable in external data before we ever finance a supplier, so we can screen new leads on them.

Every success factor above can be estimated from customs records and 1688 / marketplace data at the lead stage:

Shipment frequency → count of shipments/month Invoice structure → typical shipment value Category → product listings Momentum → shipment trend quarter-on-quarter Product breadth → number of distinct products shipped Buyer quality → is the buyer a major US retailer Country & export → origin + is it an exporter

The logic for prioritizing outreach follows directly from the patterns:

StepLogic
ExcludeWrong vertical (components, apparel), domestic-only flow, and the big-ticket / single-buyer / low-frequency "one-shot" profile (96% churn).
PrioritizeThe seven winning-profile factors: high shipment frequency · small-ticket structure · strong category (textile / decor / tableware) · rising trend · broad product range · major US-retailer buyer · exporter (India / HK strongest). The more a lead hits — weighted by what matters in its category — the higher the priority.
NurtureRight category but low current activity — keep warm, act when their shipment trend turns up.
SequenceWithin the priority group, contact the largest estimated volume first — size decides ordering, after convertibility qualifies the lead.
Guiding principle: convertibility first, size second. We qualify a lead on how it trades, then use estimated volume only to decide who to call first. The exact scoring can be defined later — this report is about the pattern and the logic.
Prepared for internal BD review. Stickiness = share of clients still active at data cut-off or with 12+ months of continuous activity; churn rate = the remainder (100% − stickiness). All figures derived from the onboarded-client financing record (Jan 2024 – Jun 2026); they describe value and stickiness after onboarding. A separate exercise (tracking leads we pursue but do not win) would be needed to measure raw conversion likelihood.
