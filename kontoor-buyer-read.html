Kontoor Brands — Buyer, Market & Supply-Chain Read

Where Kontoor actually earns its money · what it buys and who makes it · how its category is moving in the US and the EU · how it compares with Levi's and the rest · and what our own customs data gets right and wrong

The main point. Kontoor is not a three-brand apparel group; it is Wrangler with attachments. In FY2025 Wrangler was 61% of revenue but 81% of segment profit, at a 23.0% segment margin against Lee's 9.2% — and Lee has now been sold. What remains is a business that is 85% wholesale, 72% United States, and 30% Walmart, selling one product: a single customs heading is 87% of everything it imports. That product's market is genuinely growing — US apparel retail is running +5.9% and Kontoor has taken share for 17 straight quarters — but Europe is not: EU clothing turnover was −4.2% in Q1 2026. And on sourcing, our own customs sample is wrong. It says Pakistan 44% and Bangladesh 27%. Kontoor discloses Bangladesh 57% and Pakistan 4%, and the US and EU market data agree with Kontoor. Finding that out — and knowing why — is the most valuable thing in this document.

01 · WHERE THE MONEY IS

Wrangler is 61% of the revenue and 81% of the profit

How to read. Both bars are shares of the FY2025 total, so each colour sums to 100%. Blue is each brand's share of revenue; orange is its share of the company's total segment profit. When the orange bar is longer than the blue one, that brand is carrying the others.

Wrangler earns 20 points more of the profit than it does of the revenue. Lee earns 11 points less. That single gap is the entire logic of the Lee divestiture, whatever the press release says about growth profile.

The trendThe gap did not appear suddenly — it widened for three years
  • Wrangler's segment margin went 17.5% → 20.3% → 23.0% across FY2023–25, and reached 28.8% in the first half of 2026. Lee's went 11.6% → 11.3% → 9.2%, and in Q4 2025 alone it was 3.7%. The spread widened from 5.9 points to 13.8 points in two years.
  • Lee's revenue fell every year — $842m, $791m, $750m — but its profit fell faster, down 30% over two years while Wrangler's rose 43%. A shrinking brand losing margin at the same time is a brand with no operating leverage left.
  • Helly Hansen is not yet a profit engine. It contributed 15% of FY2025 revenue and 6% of segment profit, at a 6.9% margin on a seven-month stub. It improved to 7.9% in H1 2026 and management calls it "the next billion-dollar-plus brand", but it is being bought forward, not harvested.
  • One caveatSegment profit is a GAAP number and it includes the IEEPA tariff refunds — Kontoor booked a $54m net receivable in Q1 2026 which cut cost of goods sold by $49m. Kontoor does not publish an adjusted segment profit, so the clean Wrangler margin is not disclosed. The direction is unambiguous; the exact level for 2026 is not.

  • Direct-to-consumer rose from 11.9% to 15.2% of revenue over three years, almost all of it from the Helly Hansen acquisition. Strip Helly Hansen out and Wrangler is 10.0% direct — it sells through other people's stores.
  • International went from 20.0% to 27.4% of revenue in FY2025, again driven by Helly Hansen, which is 75.6% international. Wrangler is 10.9% international. Kontoor's international story is a Helly Hansen story.
  • Customer concentration is the real exposureWalmart was 30% of FY2025 revenue and the top ten customers were 53%. A year earlier those figures were 36% and 62% — the fall is dilution from the Helly Hansen acquisition, not a reduction in Walmart volume. Post-Lee, on a Wrangler-heavy base, Walmart's share of continuing revenue is materially higher than 30%, and Kontoor does not disclose it.
  • An unnamed second customer is 12% of revenue. Kontoor names Target, Amazon, Boot Barn, Tractor Supply, Cavender's and — new for the second half of 2026 — Lowe's as accounts, but does not attach percentages to any of them.
  • What this means for a supplier. A factory shipping to Kontoor is, at one remove, shipping to Walmart. Walmart's order cadence, markdown policy and inventory posture set Kontoor's, and Kontoor's set the factory's. That is a chain worth pricing, and it is not visible in any rating.

03 · WHAT IT BUYS

One customs heading is 87% of everything it imports

How to read. Share of total declared units across 1,528 product lines. One four-digit heading dominates so completely that the rest are barely visible — that is the finding, not a scaling problem.

HS 6203 — men's and boys' woven trousers, shorts and suits — is 87.2% of all units. Womenswear is 3.1%. Fabric, shirts, knitwear and hangers are each under 2%. Kontoor's supply risk is not apparel risk; it is denim-trouser risk, and after the Lee sale it is Wrangler denim-trouser risk.

FibreWhat it is made of

Share of units whose description names the fibre. Descriptions often name several, so these do not sum to 100%.

Buying entityWho places the order

Nine buyer name variants in the file resolve to four Kontoor legal blocks.

  • Cotton is named in 87% of units, denim in 79%, and elastane or spandex in 78%. Stretch denim is the standard product, not rigid denim. That is why the mills Kontoor uses are vertically integrated spinners rather than cut-and-sew shops — elastane is a separate imported input with its own lead time and its own working-capital need.
  • Kontoor states it manufactures or sources roughly 157 million units a year from about 330 contract factories across 28 countries, and that its top three North American suppliers provide about 46% of raw materials. The raw-material concentration is higher than the factory concentration.
  • Ordering splits across four legal blocks: Kontoor US and Brands Inc. 60%, Kontoor International (Switzerland) 19%, Kontoor Asia Trading (Hong Kong) 17%, Kontoor Canada 4%. A supplier invoicing the Swiss entity faces a different counterparty, jurisdiction and payment pattern from one invoicing the US entity. Any financing has to be written against the right one, and the raw file will not tell you which without resolving the names.

04 · THE MARKET — UNITED STATES

The category is growing. Most of the growth is price

How to read. Both lines are rebased to 2021 = 100 so they can share an axis. Blue is the dollar value of US clothing-store retail sales. Orange is the consumer price index for apparel. The distance between them is real volume.

US clothing retail sales are running +5.9% in 2026 and 2025 was the best year since 2022 — but apparel prices turned from flat to +3.9% year-on-year over the same period. Roughly two thirds of the growth is price. Real volume growth is around 2%.

  • Apparel price inflation was zero to slightly negative through 2023–2025 and flipped sharply in 2026 — the index rose 2.8% in the first seven months of the year alone, peaking at +4.8% year-on-year in May. That is tariff and margin pass-through, not input cost: over the same period the landed unit value of US HS 6203 imports fell 6.5%, from $8.92 to $8.34 per garment.
  • The denim category specificallyEvery operator says it is accelerating. Levi's chief executive Michelle Gass, July 2026: "the category is projected to grow mid-single digits annually through 2030, outpacing the category's historical growth", and in April: "The denim category remains healthy. It's actually accelerating here in the U.S. It's outperforming overall apparel."
  • Kontoor's own numbersWrangler bottoms point-of-sale is up "low to mid-single digit" and +3% year to date through July — while gaining more than 100 basis points of share and recording its 17th consecutive quarter of share gains. A brand taking share that fast, growing only 3%, implies the underlying US bottoms category is roughly flat to low-single-digit in units. The "mid-single digits to 2030" figure is a projection, not an observation.
  • And the shipments are weaker than the sell-through. Q2 2026 Wrangler global revenue grew 2% and US wholesale was flat despite the share gains, because retailers are not restocking. Chief financial officer Joe Alkire: "Retailers just remain very cautious… Inventory levels at retail were down high single, if not low double digits" and he would "continue to characterize inventory at retail as suboptimal."
  • For a supplierFlat wholesale shipments against growing sell-through is a coiled spring, but it is also a working-capital squeeze that lands on the factory, not on Kontoor. The retailer holds less stock, Kontoor holds less stock, and the buffer moves upstream.

Europe is not doing the same thing, and Kontoor says so itself

Supply sideEURATEX, Q1 2026 vs Q1 2025

EURATEX Economic Update, Q1 2026 — the European apparel and textile industry association, so a trade body rather than a statistical office.

  • EU retail volume is 15% above 2021 but has stalled — the 2026 first half sits just 1.2% above the 2025 annual average and about 2% above the same half of 2025, and June came in below the March peak. This is not the US picture.
  • EU clothing turnover fell 4.2% and production 5.1% in Q1 2026, the third consecutive year of contraction for the European textile and clothing industry. EURATEX's own words: "Weak EU domestic demand, intensified competition from low-cost imports, and a subdued global trade environment remained the dominant headwinds" and "no signs of stabilisation."
  • Kontoor agreesJoe Alkire, Q2 2026: "Europe was flattish to down right now, and we expect that to turn relative to the year coming in." That is notably cooler than chief executive Scott Baxter one quarter earlier: "There's a real denim movement going on in Europe, and Wrangler is a huge part of that." Two quarters, two different European stories from the same company.
  • The import data says something different again. Extra-EU imports of men's cotton trousers grew 4.8% in value from 2023 to 2025, to €5.48bn — larger than the US market for the same product ($4.42bn). European demand for the category is not collapsing; European production is. Imports are taking the share.
  • SourcingChina is gaining share in the EU while losing it in the US. In men's cotton trousers, China went from 7.15% to 8.09% of extra-EU imports while falling from 6.44% to 3.41% of US imports. That is tariff-driven trade diversion, visible in the data, and it is the clearest single piece of evidence that the two markets have decoupled.

06 · KONTOOR vs PEERS

Levi's keeps 15 more points of gross margin and turns less of it into profit

How to read. Blue is gross margin, orange is operating margin, both on each company's own adjusted basis for its most recent full financial year. The distance between the two bars is what the company spends to sell the product. Where the bars are close together, the business is efficient; where they are far apart, it is paying for stores and marketing.

Levi's earns 61.7% gross margin and keeps 11.4% at the operating line. Kontoor earns 46.6% and keeps 14.9%. Fifteen points of gross margin disappear into the cost of running a direct-to-consumer estate. The ranking inverts completely between the two lines, and the operating line is the one that pays interest.

  • The gap is a channel structure, not a sourcing advantage. Levi's is 50.8% direct-to-consumer and captures the retail markup. Kontoor is 85% wholesale and hands that markup to Walmart. Levi's is one third price-driven growth and talks openly about "premiumization"; Wrangler sits at opening-to-mid price points in mass channels.
  • Kontoor's sourcing is arguably the better one. Kontoor discloses 2% China; Levi's has roughly 15% of its factories there and assumes a 30% China tariff in guidance. Kontoor's lower gross margin is a distribution outcome, not a manufacturing one.
  • Tariff dragLevi's: about 150 basis points of gross margin, "fully offsetting this with higher pricing… we're not seeing any initial demand reaction", with $80m of refunds received and excluded from guidance. American Eagle: 150–200 basis points, $190m claimed and $140m net expected. Kontoor gives no dollar figure at all — the FY2025 10-K says only that tariffs "impacted gross margins during 2025 and are expected to continue." VF quantifies nothing either.
  • Gildan is the control case. It owns its factories, earns only 34.5% gross margin, and converts that into 22.3% operating margin — the highest here. Levi's spends about 50 points of gross margin to sell; Gildan spends about 12. Same industry, inverted cost structures. Vertical integration buys operating leverage, not gross margin.
  • Two peers goneGuess? was taken private by Authentic Brands Group in January 2026 and files nothing after Q3 FY2026. Hanesbrands was acquired by Gildan in December 2025. And Authentic Brands now owns both Guess and Lee — a single licensor sitting behind two of the names on this page. For anyone tracking counterparties, that is a concentration that did not exist a year ago.

07 · THE OFF-PRICE ANGLE

Off-price says merchandise is "off the charts". The denim brands say their inventory is tight

How to read. Same-store or comparable sales growth in the most recent reported quarter, for the three US off-price retailers and for the two branded denim wholesalers. All are calendar Q1–Q2 2026.

Ross grew comparable sales 17%, TJX and Burlington 6% each — while Wrangler's US wholesale was flat and Levi's global wholesale grew 3%. The off-price channel is absorbing enormous volumes of apparel. It is not coming from these two.

  • Off-priceTJX chief executive Ernie Herrman, May 2026: "Availability of merchandise is off the charts" and "the bigger we have become, the more availability we see." Ross chief executive James Conroy: "The availability of closeouts in the marketplace is still outstanding… we are getting a lot of first calls now." Burlington's Michael O'Sullivan: "The supply of off-price merchandise is excellent right now."
  • The brandsLevi's ended its latest quarter with inventory down 7% and "continued progress in reducing excess and obsolete". Kontoor reports retail inventories down high-single to low-double digits. Neither is feeding the off-price channel; both are running tight.
  • These are not contradictory statements. They say the glut is elsewhere in apparel — and that a branded denim maker with tight inventory is not the marginal supplier to off-price. Ross's packaway inventory fell from 41% to 36% of the total while comparable sales rose 17%: goods are going straight to the shop floor rather than into storage. Supply is being absorbed, not stockpiled.
  • Kontoor's own clearance estateThe words "off-price", TJX, Ross and Burlington do not appear in the FY2025 10-K. What it does disclose is its own clearance estate: 72 company-operated outlet and clearance centres, up from 57 a year earlier — a 26% increase in owned clearance capacity in one year. Kontoor is building its own outlet for excess rather than selling it into third-party off-price, which protects brand and margin but keeps the markdown risk on its own books.
  • For a supplierA brand that clears through its own outlets rather than dumping to off-price is a brand that manages order books tightly and does not place speculative volume. That is good for credit quality and bad for factory utilisation. It is also exactly why Kontoor's wholesale shipments can be flat while the category grows.

08 · WHERE IT IS MADE

Our customs sample says Pakistan. Kontoor and the market both say Bangladesh

How to read. Four independent readings of the same question — which countries make this product. Blue is our own customs sample. Orange is Kontoor's published sourcing mix. Green and yellow are the whole US and EU import markets for men's cotton trousers in 2025. If our bar disagrees with the other three, our bar is the one that is wrong.

Our sample puts Pakistan at 44% and Bangladesh at 27%. Kontoor publishes Bangladesh 57% and Pakistan 4%. The US market is Bangladesh 36% and Pakistan 9%; the EU market is Bangladesh 37% and Pakistan 18%. Three sources agree with each other and disagree with us. Sri Lanka, 8% of our sample, sits inside Kontoor's undisclosed 9% "Other" and is left off the chart.

We tested this before publishing it, and our own data lost. The customs sample is not a measurement of Kontoor's sourcing mix. It is a measurement of which countries publish export manifests. Below is the evidence, because a number this wrong is worth more than a number that is quietly right.
  • The Americas holeKontoor discloses 27% Americas and Mexico is the second-largest supplier of men's cotton trousers to the US at 16.05% of the market. Our sample shows 0.5%. There is no sourcing story that reconciles that. There is a very well-documented data story: US law restricts public bill-of-lading records to vessel shipments only, and trade with Mexico moves by truck and rail. Mexico is structurally invisible to this kind of data.
  • Who publishes manifestsPakistan, India and Sri Lanka are 69% of our sample — and are three of the four apparel-exporting countries in the standard bill-of-lading universe. Bangladesh, Vietnam, Cambodia, Indonesia, Egypt and Jordan do not publish export manifests. Bangladesh appears in our data only through the import side; Pakistan, India and Sri Lanka appear through both sides. That asymmetry roughly doubles them relative to Bangladesh, which is close to exactly what we observe.
  • RenormalisingTake Kontoor's disclosure, remove the Americas entirely and rescale the rest: Bangladesh becomes 78% and Pakistan 5.5%. Even under the most generous adjustment we cannot recover Pakistan at 44%. Something is adding Pakistan volume, not merely removing Americas volume.
  • A cotton-only nodePakistan is 9.4% of US imports of cotton trousers and outside the top fifteen for synthetic-fibre trousers. A query filtered to a denim buyer lands disproportionately on precisely the flows where Pakistan is most visible. Pakistan is also twice as important to the EU (17.9%) as to the US (9.4%) — and an exporter-side manifest catches its EU-bound shipments too, inflating it a second time.
  • The buyer corroboratesKontoor states it uses US-grown cotton in more than 80% of products sourced from Bangladesh and is pursuing a duty exemption on that basis. A company builds a cotton-traceability programme around its largest sourcing country, not its third. That independently supports the 57% figure.
What actually changedKontoor closed its Mexican plants and moved the work to Bangladesh
  • The FY2024 10-K describes seven owned manufacturing facilities in Mexico supplying about 30% of units. The FY2025 10-K puts owned Mexico at 23% of units. In September 2025 Kontoor announced the closure of four Mexican plants affecting more than 2,000 workers, with production relocating to Bangladesh. Today the company's supply-chain page lists three owned facilities.
  • Over the same period the contract base went from about 210 factories in 19 countries to about 330 in 28 — partly Helly Hansen, partly tariff-driven diversification.
  • This is the single most consequential supply-chain event at this buyer in two years, and our customs sample cannot see any of it, because the plants that closed were in the country the data does not cover.
Market shiftWho is winning and losing this category, 2023 to 2025
  • Bangladesh is the structural winner in both markets — the only large supplier gaining materially, up 3.7 points in the US and 1.4 in the EU. China lost 3.0 points in the US and gained 0.9 in the EU. Türkiye is the big EU loser, down 2.9 points: nearshoring is losing to Bangladesh and Pakistan on cost.
  • Unit values explain the direction. In 2025 a men's cotton trouser landed in the US at $6.03 from Bangladesh, $7.62 from Pakistan and $10.86 from Mexico. Category-wide landed value fell 6.5% from 2023 to 2025. The category is deflating at the border and inflating at the till.
  • Where the tariff arbitrage sitsBangladesh secured a 19% reciprocal tariff in February 2026 with a carve-out mechanism taking apparel made with US-produced cotton to zero. Kontoor has put 57% of its sourcing in that country and claims over 80% US-cotton content there. Whether by design or luck, it is the best-positioned sourcing book in this peer set.

It can still do the thing bill-of-lading data is actually built for — identify individual factories, their products, their destinations and their relative size within the visible set. Everything below is scoped to that, and to the 58 contract manufacturers we can see.

Because Bangladesh and the Americas are under-represented, true concentration across Kontoor's ~330 factories is lower than this, not higher. Read these as an upper bound.

10 · THREE LENSES

The same file gives three different answers. Two of them are wrong

How to read. Identical data, identical suppliers, three ways of measuring "how much". If a country's three bars disagree, the metric is doing the talking, not the supply chain.

Pakistan is 87% of the visible programme by declared value, 44% by volume and 17% by number of shipments. Bangladesh is 27% by volume and 0% by value. Bangladesh does not vanish because Kontoor stopped buying there. It vanishes because the source carries no declared value for it.

  • Ten of the fifteen origin countries carry no declared value whatsoever, and Sri Lanka carries it on 3% of records. Value exists for Pakistan, India and Mexico and effectively nowhere else.
  • The consequence is not cosmetic. On value this looks like a dangerously concentrated buyer: HHI 2,009, top five 87%, only 19 suppliers visible. On volume it is HHI 674, top five 47%, 58 suppliers. One of those two fails a concentration screen; the other passes comfortably.
  • Counting shipments is the opposite error — HHI 304 — because a Bangladeshi supplier sending many small consignments outranks a Pakistani mill sending few large ones.
What we do about it. Volume — declared containers and packages — is the only field populated consistently across all fifteen origins, so every reliance figure here is volume-based. Declared value is used only to say whether a value exists at all. A dashboard that silently sums the value column will report Pakistan at 87% and never flag that Bangladesh is missing.

12 · HS CODES vs DESCRIPTIONS

The HS code does not tell you what the product is

How to read. Within HS heading 6203, blue is each six-digit line's share of volume and orange is the share of that line's own descriptions that describe womenswear. If a code labelled "men's" is full of women's descriptions, the code is not the product.

Two thirds of Kontoor's denim-trouser volume — 58% of everything it imports — sits under HS 620322, which formally means "men's or boys' cotton ensembles", and 42% of the descriptions under it say women's, ladies' or girls'. The code that actually means men's cotton trousers, 620342, holds under a third of the volume and its descriptions are 96% men's.

  • This is not an error, it is how origin customs regimes classify. The 620322 lines carry Pakistani export-scheme references and mix men's and women's denim pants under one heading. Anyone deriving product mix from HS codes alone will report Kontoor as buying ensembles and undercount womenswear by roughly a factor of ten.
  • 314 of 1,528 lines carry no HS code at all — 3.3% of units — yet still contain a usable description: fibre content, gender, wash, purchase-order and style numbers.
  • Parsing the text recovers fibre, gender, garment type and — in 203 lines — brand names: Wrangler in 179, Lee in 21, Riders in 5. That is only 1.7% of units, not enough to size brands. It is enough to prove brand-level attribution is extractable, which is exactly the question a Lee divestiture makes urgent for a supplier.
  • The same text carries carton counts, gross weight, cubic metres, notify parties and purchase-order numbers — the fields that let us tell a real capacity shift from a paperwork change.
